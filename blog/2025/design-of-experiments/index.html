(Bioassay) Experimental Design to Determine Drug Toxicity 🧪

Created on March 26, 2025

2025   ·   DesignOfExperiments   Statistics   ·   my-posts

This experimental design was part of a preclinical Bioassay, but it can be adapted to clinical settings. The goal of this design was to determine if it was possible to administer a higher dosage to a drug without toxic side effects. This experiment involved the use of intravitreal injections to deliver drug “X” at different dosages as treatments on the experimental units. Drug “X” is administered every 4 weeks.

Objective

To determine the dose-dependent whether there is toxicity of drug “X” in a controlled population of the experimental units.

In this experiment, the following responses were measured;

Quantity Description
Length of Rod Outer Segments \((\mu m)\) A measure of retinal detachment
Retinal Thickness \((\mu m)\) A measure of photoreceptor function
“Y” levels in vitreous humor \((pg/ml)\) An inflammation marker as a measure of toxicity
Mortality rate Death to life ratio of experimental units between the beginning and end of the experiment
Weight change Weight lost or gained by the experimental units after administering drug

Although multiple response variables were measured, in this blog I only discuss the toxicity level (Y levels in vitreous humor) measured in picograms per milliliter \((pg/ml)\). \(pg/ml\)s is a unit of measurement for concentration of a substance in a fluid sample. A picogram is one-trillionth of a gram, and a milliliter is one-thousandth of a liter.

Methodology
  • Blocking: Controlled for 3 age groups (A1, A2, and A3) in the experimental units at the beginning of the experiment.
  • Randomization: Randomly assigned experimental units to each treatment group to minimize bias.
  • Blinding: Double-blinded the treatment administration and evaluation process.
  • Treatment Administration: Single dosage once every 4 weeks.
  • Monitoring:
    • Daily observation was for physical/behavioral changes.
    • Weekly body weight was measured.
    • Periodic measurement of the length of rod outer segments and retinal thickness.
    • “Y” levels in vitreous humor were measured at pre specified irregular intervals. For instance, 3hrs, 9hrs, 24hrs, 48hrs, etc. We also perform replication by independently assigning the treatments to several experimental units to ensure reproducibility of the results.
Statistical Analysis & Hypothesis Testing

Naturally, since we are interested in the concentration of “Y” (a continuous variable) at more than two treatment levels, we would use an Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) Model. Additionally, we are blocking for age of the treatment units, and so we will use a two-way ANOVA model. We could also use an Analysis of Covariance (two-way ANCOVA) model to compare differences across treatment groups to determine whether the weight change can explain any possible observed effects.

Note: If any mortality is recorded, we can use a Chi-square homogeneity test to determine whether the mortality recorded at diffrent treatment levels have similar distributions.

Using a two-way ANOVA, we define our hypothesis as follows:

Null Hypothesis (H₀): Drug “X” does not cause any toxic effects at any tested dose, after accounting for the age of the experimental units.
Alternative Hypothesis (H₁): Drug “X” exhibits toxicity at one or more of the tested dose levels even after accounting for age.

Rejecting the null hypothesis in favor of the alternate hypothesis means that there is an age-independent difference in the effect across different treatment levels. This indicates the presence of dose-dependent toxicity. Failing to reject the null hypothesis means that there is no difference in the toxicity level.

  \(\alpha_1\) 4 4 4 4 4
Age Groups \(\alpha_2\) 4 4 4 4 4

Here, I employed a randomized complete block design with a full factorial design. Also, since I chose and equal number of experimental units in each factor-level combination, we have a balanced design. However when live subjects are used in experiments, mortality may lead to a change in number of experimental units, creating an unbalanced design, and hence a reduction in the power of the test.

The Effects model of the two-way ANOVA is given by:

\[y_{ijk} = \mu + \tau_j + \alpha_k + (\tau\alpha)_{jk} + \varepsilon_{ijk}\]
  • \(\mu\) is the collective mean of all observations \(y_{ijk}\)
  • \(\tau_j\) is the effect of factor \(\tau\) at treatment \(j\)
  • \(\alpha_k\) is the effect of treatment \(\alpha\) at treatment \(k\)
  • \((\tau\alpha)_{jk}\) is the effect of interaction between the two factors
  • \(\varepsilon_{ijk}\) is the residual error of the model at each observation
  • The assumption of this model is that the residual error \(\varepsilon_{ijk}\) is normally distributed with \(0\) mean

Type II Error (\(\beta\)) is the probability of not detecting toxicity at any level when it is indeed present. Power: the probability of detecting the presence of toxicity at at least one treatment level if there is toxicity present. In this design, we desire a high power since the safety of the drug is paramount.

\[Power = 1- \beta\]

We can then perform a power analysis to estimate the sample/replication size needed to achieve the specified power.

Ethical Considerations

Since this test involves the use of live subjects as experimental units, there are some ethical guidelines to

  • Follow for use of human subjects
  • Follow Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) for use of animal subjects.
  • Minimize animal suffering.
  • Use the minimum number of animals needed to achieve statistical power.
