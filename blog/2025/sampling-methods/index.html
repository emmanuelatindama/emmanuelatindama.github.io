Sampling Secrets -- How Statisticians Find the Truth

Created on April 28, 2025

2025   ·   SamplingMethods   Statistics   ·   my-posts

We live in an age of big data. Millions of data is collected by the second all over; weather data, shared posts and likes on social media, location data on our phones, credit card transactions, etc. Storing and analyzing this data requires a lot of expensive hardware, and so it is often useful to use only a portion of the data to perform quick analysis, and then extrapolating the results to the entire population. Sampling methods therefore, are essential techniques that allow researchers to study a subset of a population and make inferences about the whole. A strong sampling strategy leads to more accurate, reliable results.

1. 🎯 Motivating Problem

A medical facility, X, stores a 2000 plasma samples in a storage facility among other supplies. It is discovered that the facility may have been exposed to contamination. What is an appropriate sample size that should be selected that would ensure reasonable confidence that the plasma store was not affected, making it safe to use?

We would explore the solution to this problem near after discussing what sampling is, why it is important, and the different types of sampling as well as why you would chose them.

2. Why Sampling?

Sampling is the method of selecting a subset of a larger population. A sample is always with respect to a larger population, not necessarily the size.

For instance, selecting all 11 players of a soccer team for a study about that team is not a sample, since everyone in the team was used. When populations are too large to study entirely, sampling offer a practical solution. Instead of gathering data from everyone, we study a representative subset to save time, cost, and effort — while still drawing meaningful conclusions. The goal of sampling is to extrapolate the results to the entire population. Therefore, the way it is done is very important. If litte Johnny asks his 3 other friends their opinion of the little league baseball coach, it is a valid sample, but not a very representative the entire team’s opinion of the coach.

“Good sampling makes reliable statistics.”

Sampling is important because;

  • It is cost-efficient and less time consuming.
  • It is particularly useful when performing destructive experiments (say, performing crash tests of a new car model).
  • It is useful when working with and indeterminate or infinite population.
A sampling illustration.

3. Types of Sampling Methods

In Statistics, sampling methods refer to how we select a subset of the pupulation. There are many different ways to select a sample depending on the objective of the researcher. Researchers commonly use simple random sampling, but this is not always the ideal approach, depending on the application. Sampling methods fall into two major categories: Probability Sampling and Non-Probability Sampling.

3.1 🎲 Probability Sampling

Probability sampling ensures each individual has a known (and often equal) chance of selection, making it statistically sound.

Simple Random Sampling 🎟️

Each element may be assigned a random number, and then using a random number generator, we may select a sample from the population based on the random numbers generated. The random number may be selected using any probability distribution function, although a normal distribution is the most common choice.

Each member of the population has an equal chance of being selected.
📌 Example 1: Picking names from a hat. 📌 Example 2: Selecting a ball from a pot containing identical balls of 3 different colors.

An illustration of simple random sampling .
Stratified Sampling 🏛️ Dividing the population into subgroups (strata) and selecting a simple random sample from each subgroup. 📌 *Example 1:* Sampling students from different academic majors.
A graphical representation of stratified sampling .
 Cluster Sampling 🏘️

Dividing the population into clusters, then randomly selecting entire clusters. Similar to stratified sampling, except that we select entire subgroups, not individual elements from the subgroups. Each element in the population has a known probability of being selected. 📌 Example: Surveying a few randomly selected neighborhoods in a city as part of a study.

Systematic Sampling 📏

Pick every k-th element from a list, starting from a random point. Systematic sampling is a form of simple random sampling. 📌 Example: Surveying every 10th visitor to a museum.

3.2 🔍 Non-Probability Sampling

Non-probability sampling does not guarantee every individual an equal chance. It’s quicker but can be biased.

Convenience Sampling ☕ Choose whoever is **easiest to reach**. 📌 *Example:* Asking nearby shoppers for feedback.
Judgmental (Purposive) Sampling 🧠 Select based on the researcher's **expert judgment**. 📌 *Example:* Interviewing industry experts only.
Snowball Sampling ❄️ Existing participants help **recruit** others. 📌 *Example:* Finding study subjects through referrals in niche communities.
Quota Sampling 📊 Ensure the sample meets **specific demographic quotas**. 📌 *Example:* 40% men, 60% women in a survey.

4. Choosing the Right Method

Choosing the right sampling method depends on:

Factor Consideration
Research Objective Accuracy vs. speed? Generalization needed?
Population Structure Homogeneous or diverse?
Resources Budget, manpower, time?
Bias Tolerance Is some bias acceptable for faster results?

✅ Tip: Use probability sampling for formal studies. Use non-probability sampling for exploratory research or pilot studies.

5. 🛠️ Solving the Motivating Problem

Let’s return to our original challenge:

Estimate the average number of exercise hours per week in a city of 500,000 people.

Example

Imagine you are a public health official tasked with estimating the average number of hours people exercise per week in a city of 500,000 residents.

😵 Interviewing every person is impossible.
How can you efficiently gather accurate, representative data without surveying everyone?

➡️ Chosen Sampling Method: Cluster Sampling 🏘️

Why?

  • The city is geographically large.
  • Residents naturally live in neighborhoods (clusters).
  • It’s more practical to randomly choose a few neighborhoods and survey everyone in those neighborhoods.

📋 Steps:

  1. Divide the city into 1,000 neighborhoods.
  2. Randomly select 10 neighborhoods.
  3. Survey all residents within the selected neighborhoods about their weekly exercise habits.
  4. Analyze the collected data to estimate the city-wide average.

🎯 By sampling clusters rather than individuals randomly scattered throughout the city, we save time and resources — while still achieving reliable estimates!

6. Conclusion

Sampling is both an art and a science.
The right technique ensures your data truly reflects your population, allowing confident, defensible decisions.

🔔 Key Takeaway:

“A well-designed sample can tell the story of a thousand data points — and sometimes, it’s all you need.”

Sampling Methods: How Big Should Your Sample Be?

Imagine you receive a delivery of 200 pieces of equipment, and you know that 5 of them are defective.
You need to sample randomly from the batch to inspect for defects.
A natural question arises:

How large should your sample be to have a high probability (say, 95%) of finding at least one defective item?

This is a classic sampling problem — let’s solve it!

Understanding the Problem

  • Total items: 200
  • Defective items: 5
  • Goal: Find the minimum sample size \(n\) needed so that the probability of detecting at least one defective is at least x%.

How to Solve It: Using Probability

The probability of not selecting a defective item in one draw is:

\[\text{Probability (no defectives in one draw)} = \frac{195}{200} = 0.975\]

If you draw \(n\) items randomly, the probability that none of them are defective is approximately:

\[\left( \frac{195}{200} \right)^n\]

Thus, the probability of finding at least one defective is:

\[1 - \left( \frac{195}{200} \right)^n\]

We want:

\[1 - \left( \frac{195}{200} \right)^n \geq x\]

Rearranging:

\[\left( \frac{195}{200} \right)^n \leq 1 - x\]

Taking the natural logarithm on both sides:

\[n \geq \frac{\ln(1 - x)}{\ln\left( \frac{195}{200} \right)}\]

Worked Example

Suppose you want a 95% chance of detecting at least one defective.

Then \(x = 0.95\), and:

\[n \geq \frac{\ln(1 - 0.95)}{\ln\left( \frac{195}{200} \right)}\]

Using a calculator:

  • \[\ln(0.05) \approx -2.9957\]
  • \[\ln(0.975) \approx -0.02532\]

Thus:

\[n \geq \frac{-2.9957}{-0.02532} \approx 118.34\]

Result:
✅ You should sample at least 119 pieces to have a 95% chance of detecting a defective item.

Summary

To achieve an \(x\%\) probability of finding at least one defective in a batch:

\[n \geq \frac{\ln(1 - x)}{\ln\left( \frac{195}{200} \right)}\]
  • Always round up to the next whole number.
  • The rarer the defectives, the larger your sample must be.

Key Takeaways

  • Sampling can provide high confidence in quality control.
  • Probabilistic reasoning is essential to determine how large your sample should be.
  • For real-world inspections, plan to sample accordingly based on the defect rate!
